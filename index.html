Arijit Ghosh

Visiting Fellow (Postdoctoral) · National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute · Bethesda, MD

I am a visiting postdoctoral fellow in Susan Harbison’s group at the Laboratory of Systems Genetics, SBC, NHLBI, NIH in Bethesda, Maryland. I study how the circadian clock and sleep genetic networks interact to shape behavior and physiology. During my PhD with Vasu Sheeba at the Chronobiology and Behavioural Neurogenetics Laboratory, JNCASR, Bengaluru, I studied genetic and molecular influences on the timing of behavior (chronotypes), both under the control of and independent from the circadian clock.

My work combines behavioral recordings in Drosophila with statistical modeling, quantitative genetics, and multi-omics. Earlier, during my integrated MSc at NISER, Bhubaneswar, I studied thermo-sensitive Transient Receptor Potential (TRP) ion channels and their role in osteogenesis using primary bone cells, rodent models, pharmacology, and molecular dynamics.

I grew up in Burdwan, West Bengal, went to school at RKMV Purulia and RKMV Narendrapur, and moved from Bhubaneswar to Bengaluru before coming to Bethesda. Outside the lab, I enjoy photography, science fiction, good food, and building small open-source R packages (including hand-drawn plots in ggsketch and Indian landscape palettes in prakriti).

Research

In the lab, I record behavior across thousands of fruit flies over days of light and darkness, then pair statistical models with quantitative genetics to ask how the circadian clock and sleep genetic networks talk to each other.

Reading an actogram

Most mornings in chronobiology start by looking at an actogram: each row plots 48 hours of a single fly’s movement, two days side by side so rhythms crossing midnight stay continuous. Under a 12:12 light–dark cycle (days 1–7), wild-type flies anticipate dawn and dusk with a quiet midday siesta in between.

When we switch the incubator to constant darkness (DD, day 8 onward), those peaks keep recurring on internal time, drifting each day according to the fly’s free-running period, τ. Inferring hidden sleep states

In standard infrared beam-break monitors, bouts of at least five minutes of immobility are conventionally scored as sleep. This useful rule groups together periods of rest that may differ in sleep depth.

Our hidden Markov models (FlyDreamR) infer four behavioral states from movement recordings, labeled active wake, quiet wake, light sleep, and deep sleep. In separate DART arousal experiments, independently fitted four-state models identified states that differed in their responses to mechanical stimulation, supporting an interpretation of graded sleep depth. These are inferred behavioral states rather than electrophysiologically defined sleep stages; we use them to investigate how genetic variation relates to sleep and waking behavior.

The animation is a generative example: a simulated hidden state sequence produces activity counts. The lower trace shows that generating sequence, not states inferred from the displayed counts. Both research figures are illustrative simulations, not experimental recordings, and the animation’s probabilities are chosen for illustration rather than fitted to data.
